\chaptericon{\faPowerOff}
\chapter{Before You Start}
\label{ch:start}

\lede{Inside this computer there is a turtle,
and the turtle will draw whatever you tell it to.}

The turtle cannot hear you.
It can only read.
So you type words to it,
and it does what the words say.

This book teaches you the words.

On the menu, pick
``Draw Pictures with Logo Turtle''
and press \key{Enter}.

The screen turns grey.
In the middle is a small triangle.
That is the turtle, seen from above.
The point of the triangle is its nose,
and it shows which way the turtle is facing.

There is already a house on the screen.
The turtle drew it while you were waiting.
Later in this book you will find out how.

At the bottom of the screen is a question mark:

\begin{code}
?
\end{code}

That is the turtle asking,
``What should I do?''

Whatever you type goes after the question mark.
When you press \key{Enter},
the turtle reads it and does it.
Then the question mark comes back,
ready for the next thing.

\section{Big Letters or Small}

This book writes the turtle's words in small letters,
like \chip{fd 50},
because small letters are easier to type.

The turtle does not mind.
\chip{FD 50} and \chip{fd 50} mean the same thing to it.

\section{Leaving Logo}

When you are finished, type:

\begin{code}
bye
\end{code}

and press \key{Enter}.
You go back to the menu.

If the turtle will not listen to you at all,
hold down \key{Ctrl} and \key{Alt}
and press \key{Backspace}.
That always takes you back to the menu.

\begin{heads}[If you make a mistake]
You will make lots of mistakes.
Everybody does.
The turtle never breaks.
The worst thing that can happen
is that it prints a message
saying it did not understand.
Chapter~\ref{ch:wrong} tells you what the messages mean.
\end{heads}

\chapter{Meet the Turtle}
\label{ch:meet}

\lede{The turtle knows four words for moving.}

\begin{commandlist}
\cmd{fd}{forward}
\cmd{bk}{back}
\cmd{rt}{turn right}
\cmd{lt}{turn left}
\end{commandlist}

Every one of them needs a number after it.

\chip{fd 100} means ``go forward 100 steps.''
The turtle's steps are tiny,
so 100 of them is not very far.

\chip{rt 90} means ``turn right.''
The 90 says how much to turn.
Chapter~\ref{ch:turn} is all about that number.

The turtle has a pen in its belly.
Wherever it walks, it draws a line.

Type these three lines.
Press \key{Enter} after each one.

\example{first-steps}

The turtle walked up,
turned to face right,
and walked a little way.
The line it left behind is the drawing.

Now look at the turtle itself.
Its nose points to the right.
The turtle always remembers
where it is and which way it is facing.
\chip{fd} and \chip{bk} change where it is.
\chip{rt} and \chip{lt} change which way it faces.

\section{Starting Over}

Two more words:

\begin{commandlist}
\cmd{cs}{clear the screen, and put the turtle back in the middle}
\cmd{home}{put the turtle back in the middle, but keep the drawing}
\end{commandlist}

\chip{cs} is short for ``clear screen.''
You will type it a lot.

\begin{heads}[If the turtle does not do it]
The turtle wants every word spelled exactly right,
with a space before the number.

\chip{fd 50} works.
\chip{fd50} does not,
because the turtle cannot see where the word ends
and the number begins.

Look at what you typed.
Fix the one letter or the one space that is wrong,
and press \key{Enter} again.
\end{heads}

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item \chip{bk 100}. Does the turtle draw when it walks backwards?
\item \chip{rt 90} four times in a row. Which way is it facing now?
\item Draw the letter L. Then draw the letter T.
  You will need \chip{bk} for the T.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\chapter{Turning}
\label{ch:turn}

\lede{The number after \chip{rt} says how far round to turn.}

\chip{rt 90} is one corner.
It is the turn the turtle makes
at the corner of a square.

\chip{rt 180} is two corners.
The turtle ends up facing the opposite way,
as if it turned round to look behind it.

\chip{rt 360} is four corners.
That is all the way round.
The turtle spins in a circle
and ends up facing exactly the way it started.

Small numbers are small turns.
\chip{rt 45} is half a corner.
\chip{rt 10} is only a nudge.

\chip{lt} works the same way,
but the other direction.

Try this.
Each turn here is half a corner,
so the line zigs and zags.

\example{zigzag}

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item Change every \chip{50} to \chip{30}. Then to \chip{80}.
\item Change the \chip{45} at the top to \chip{20}. What changed?
\item Make the zigzag longer by typing more lines.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\chaptericon{\faIcon{sync-alt}}
\chapter{Saying It Again}
\label{ch:repeat}

\lede{Here is a square, the long way.}

\example[1.3in]{square-long}

Look at what you typed.
It is the same two lines, four times over.

The turtle has a word for ``do this again.''
It is \chip{repeat}.
After \chip{repeat} you say how many times.
Then you put the things to repeat
inside square brackets: \chip{[} and \chip{]}.

\example[1.3in]{square-repeat}

This draws the very same square.
The brackets show the turtle
where the repeated part starts and stops.

\section{The Magic Number}

A square has four corners,
and the turtle turns \chip{90} at each one.
Four times 90 is 360.
That is all the way round,
which is why the turtle ends up
facing the same way it started.

Every closed shape works like this.
The turns always add up to 360.

A triangle has three corners.
Three turns that add up to 360
are 120 each.

\example[1.2in]{triangle}

A hexagon has six corners.
Six turns that add up to 360
are 60 each.

\example[1.2in]{hexagon}

Here is the whole table.
The two numbers in each row
always multiply to make 360.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrl}
\toprule
sides & turn & name \\
\midrule
3 & 120 & triangle \\
4 & 90 & square \\
5 & 72 & pentagon \\
6 & 60 & hexagon \\
8 & 45 & octagon \\
12 & 30 & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last row has no name.
Guess what it will look like before you try it.

\example[1.2in]{twelve-sides}

\section{Round}

Twelve sides is nearly round.
What about 360 sides?
The turtle takes 360 tiny steps
and 360 tiny turns,
and the corners disappear.

\example[1.2in]{circle}

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item \chip{repeat 4 [fd 100 rt 90]} but change \chip{90} to \chip{91}.
  Then \chip{repeat 40} of that.
\item \chip{repeat 5 [fd 100 rt 144]} draws a star.
  Why does 144 work when a pentagon needs 72?
\item \chip{repeat 360 [fd 2 rt 1]} is a bigger circle.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\label{ch:pen}

\lede{The turtle can lift its pen off the paper.}

\begin{commandlist}
\cmd{pu}{pen up}
\cmd{pd}{pen down}
\end{commandlist}

With the pen up, the turtle still walks,
but it leaves no line.
Put the pen down and it draws again.

That is how you draw two things
that do not touch each other.

\example[1.2in]{dashes}

\section{Colours}

\chip{setpc} sets the pen colour.
It is short for ``set pen colour.''
Every colour has a number.

\chip{setpensize} makes the line thicker.
It wants two numbers in brackets.
Keep them the same, like \chip{[5 5]}.

\example[1.3in]{colors}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rlrl}
\toprule
0 & black   & 8  & brown \\
1 & blue    & 9  & tan \\
2 & green   & 10 & forest green \\
3 & light blue & 11 & aqua \\
4 & red     & 12 & salmon \\
5 & magenta & 13 & purple \\
6 & yellow  & 14 & orange \\
7 & white   & 15 & grey \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\chip{setbg} changes the colour of the whole screen.
It uses the same numbers.
The screen starts out grey, which is 15,
with a black pen, which is 0.

\section{Counting Inside a Repeat}

Inside a \chip{repeat},
the turtle keeps count of which time it is on.
The word for that count is \chip{repcount}.
The first time round, \chip{repcount} is 1.
The second time it is 2.
And so on.

If you use \chip{repcount} as the pen colour,
every line gets a new colour.

\example[1.3in]{color-wheel}

\section{Filling In}

\chip{fill} pours colour into a shape.

First draw the shape.
Then lift the pen
and walk the turtle inside.
Then say \chip{fill}.
The colour spreads out
until it reaches the lines.

\example[1.2in]{fill}

If the turtle is outside the shape,
the colour goes outside instead.
So make sure it is standing inside first.

\item A rainbow hexagon: \chip{repeat 6 [setpc repcount fd 60 rt 60]}
\item Type \chip{cs}. The house comes back when you open Logo again.

\end{itemize}

\chaptericon{\faGraduationCap}
\chapter{Teaching the Turtle a New Word}
\label{ch:to}

\lede{The turtle only knows the words it was born with.
But you can teach it more.}

Teaching a word is like giving a lesson.
\chip{to square} says
``here is a lesson about a word called square.''
The lines after it are what the word means.
\chip{end} says the lesson is over.

\example[1.3in]{square-word}

While you are teaching,
the question mark goes away.
Instead you see this:

\begin{code}
>
\end{code}

That is the turtle saying
``I am listening to the lesson.
I will not do these things yet.''

After you type \chip{end},
the question mark comes back.
Now \chip{square} is a word the turtle knows,
just like \chip{fd} or \chip{rt}.
Type it and the turtle draws a square.
Turn the turtle and type it again
for a tilted square.

\section{Words About Words}

\begin{commandlist}[leftmargin=0.36\linewidth,labelwidth=0.33\linewidth]
\cmd{po "square}{show the lesson for square}
\cmd{pots}{list every word you have taught}
\cmd{erase "square}{make the turtle forget square}
\end{commandlist}

Look at the quote mark in \chip{po "square}.
It is only at the front, with nothing at the end.
That looks strange, but it is right.

The quote mark tells the turtle
``I mean the name square, not the drawing.''
Without it, the turtle would draw a square
instead of showing you the lesson.

\section{Remembering}

When you leave Logo,
the turtle forgets every word you taught it.

To keep them, type:

\begin{code}
save "mine.lg
\end{code}

The turtle writes all your words down
under the name \chip{mine.lg}.

Next time you open Logo, type:

\begin{code}
load "mine.lg
\end{code}

and every word comes back.

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item Teach the turtle \chip{triangle}. Then \chip{hexagon}.
\item \chip{repeat 8 [square rt 45]}
\item Teach it \chip{box}: a square, and then walk back to where it started,
  facing the same way. Check it with \chip{repeat 3 [box fd 120]}.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\chaptericon{\faHashtag}
\chapter{Words That Take a Number}
\label{ch:inputs}

\lede{\chip{fd} takes a number. Why can't \chip{square}?}

It can.

\example[1.4in]{square-size}

Look at the first line: \chip{to square :size}.

The \chip{:size} part means
``square wants a number,
and inside the lesson we will call it size.''

The two dots in front of \chip{size} are important.
They mean ``the number that was handed to me.''
So \chip{fd :size} means
``go forward by whatever number I was given.''

Then \chip{square 30} draws a small square,
and \chip{square 120} draws a big one.
It is the same lesson every time,
with a different number.

\section{Words Inside Words}

A word you taught the turtle
can be used inside another lesson.

\example[1.5in]{flower}

\chip{flower} draws twelve squares,
turning a little between each one.
It hands its own number on to \chip{square},
so a big flower is made of big squares.

Twelve turns of 30 make 360.
That is the magic number again,
which is why the flower comes all the way round.

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item \chip{flower 30}. Then \chip{flower 120}.
\item In \chip{flower}, change \chip{12} to \chip{6} and \chip{30} to \chip{60}.
  Then try numbers that do not multiply to 360, and see.
\item Teach \chip{triangle :size} and make a flower out of triangles.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\chapter{Building Big Things}
\label{ch:house}

\lede{Big pictures are made of small words.}

A house is a square with a triangle on top.
You already know how to teach both of those.
The tricky part is getting the roof to sit flat.

\example[1.5in]{house}

Read the lesson for \chip{house} one line at a time,
and follow the turtle with your finger.

First it draws the square.
Then \chip{fd :size} walks it up the left wall,
to the top corner.
Then \chip{rt 30} tilts it a little,
so the triangle leans over the square
instead of standing up beside it.
It draws the triangle.
Then \chip{lt 30} and \chip{bk :size}
walk it back down to where it started.

This is the house that is on the screen
when you open Logo.
Now you know how it got there.

\section{A Street}

The last two lines of \chip{house} matter.
They put the turtle back exactly where it began,
facing the same way.

That means you can draw a house,
walk to the right,
and draw another one.

\example[1in]{street}

Between the houses,
the pen is up,
so the turtle leaves no line
on the way to the next one.

\begin{trythis}[Try]

\item A door. It is a small tall rectangle.
  Where does the turtle have to walk before it draws it?
\item A window. A chimney. A sun in the sky, using the circle from

\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\chaptericon{\faInfinity}
\chapter{A Word That Uses Itself}
\label{ch:recursion}

\lede{This is the strangest trick in the book, and the best.}

\example[1.5in]{spiral}

Look at the last line of the lesson.
\chip{spiral} uses \chip{spiral}.
The word calls itself.

Here is what happens.
\chip{spiral 10} walks forward 10 and turns a corner.
Then it says \chip{spiral 20}.
That walks forward 20 and turns a corner.
Then it says \chip{spiral 30}.
Each side is a little longer than the last,
so the line winds outwards.

\section{The Stop Rule}

Now look at the second line:
\chip{if :size > 150 [stop]}.

This is the stop rule.
It says
``if the number is bigger than 150, stop here.''
Without it, the turtle would never stop.
It would keep making bigger and bigger sides for ever.

Every word that uses itself needs a stop rule.
Always write the stop rule first.

Here is the same trick with triangles.

\example[1.5in]{triangle-spiral}

\begin{heads}[If the turtle will not stop]
Sometimes a program goes on and on.
Move the mouse to the very top of the window
and click on the word \textbf{Logo}.
A little list drops down.
Click \textbf{Stop}.

The question mark comes back,
and the turtle is ready for you again.

If that does not work,
hold down \key{Ctrl} and \key{Alt}
and press \key{Backspace}.
That leaves Logo and goes back to the menu.
\end{heads}

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item In \chip{spiral}, change \chip{rt 90} to \chip{rt 91}. Then to \chip{rt 89}.
\item Change \chip{+ 10} to \chip{+ 3}.
\item Change \chip{150} to \chip{300}.
\item Teach a \chip{hexspiral} that turns \chip{rt 60}.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\lede{The turtle can pick a number by itself.}

\chip{random 30} gives you a surprise number.
It might be 0. It might be 29.
It might be anything in between.
It is never 30 itself.
The number you give \chip{random}
is one more than the biggest it will pick.

Type \chip{print random 10} five times.
You get a different number nearly every time.

A drawing with \chip{random} in it
comes out different every time you draw it.

\example[1.4in]{wander}

Every step is a surprise length,

\example[1.4in]{random-colors}

and adding 1 makes it 1 to 6.

\chapter{Driving the Turtle}
\label{ch:drive}

\lede{This one is a game. You steer the turtle with the keys.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\toprule
\key{W} & forward \\
\key{S} & back \\
\key{A} & turn left \\
\key{D} & turn right \\
\key{Q} & quit \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\example[1.3in]{drive-interactive}

Here is how it works.

\chip{readchar} waits for you to press one key,
and hands that key over.
\chip{make "key} puts the key into a box called \chip{key}.
Then \chip{:key} looks in the box.
Those are the same two dots as \chip{:size},
because they mean the same thing:
``the thing I was given.''

Each \chip{if} line checks for one letter.
If the key was \chip{w}, go forward.
If it was \chip{a}, turn left.
And so on.

The last line says \chip{drive} again,
so the turtle goes back to waiting for the next key.
It keeps doing this until you press \key{Q},
which is the stop rule.

The picture above is one drive.
Yours will look different,
because you will press different keys.

\begin{heads}[If the keys do nothing]
The turtle is checking for small letters.
Make sure Caps Lock is off.
\end{heads}

\begin{trythis}[Try]
\begin{itemize}
\item Make \key{W} go further. Make \key{A} and \key{D} turn less.
\item Add a key that lifts the pen, and one that puts it down.
\item Add a key that changes the colour.
\item Add \key{C} for \chip{cs}.
\end{itemize}
\end{trythis}

\chaptericon{\faExclamationTriangle}
\chapter{When Things Go Wrong}
\label{ch:wrong}

\lede{The turtle never breaks. It just tells you when it is confused.}

When the turtle does not understand,
it prints a message at the bottom of the screen.
The message says exactly what confused it.
These are the ones you will see most.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{1.45in}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.2in}}
\toprule
The turtle says & What it means \\
\midrule
\texttt{I don't know how to squar}
  & A word the turtle has never learned.
    Usually it is spelled wrong,
    or it is a word you have not taught yet today. \\[0.5em]
\texttt{not enough inputs to fd}
  & \texttt{fd} wanted a number and did not get one. \\[0.5em]
\texttt{You don't say what to do with 50}
  & A number with no word in front of it.
    The turtle has a 50 and no idea what it is for. \\[0.5em]
\texttt{size has no value}
  & You used \texttt{:size} outside a lesson,
    so nobody handed the turtle a size. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{When the Question Mark Does Not Come Back}

If you type a \chip{[} and forget the \chip{]},
the turtle waits quietly for the rest.
The question mark does not come back.

Type \chip{]} and press \key{Enter},
and the turtle carries on.

\section{Square or ``Square}

\chip{square} tells the turtle to draw a square.

\chip{"square} tells the turtle
you mean the name of the word.

That is why it is \chip{po "square}
and \chip{erase "square}.
You are talking about the word,
not asking for the drawing.

\begin{heads}[If the turtle will not stop]
Click \textbf{Logo} at the very top of the window,
then click \textbf{Stop}.

If that does not work, hold down \key{Ctrl} and \key{Alt}
and press \key{Backspace} to go back to the menu.
\end{heads}
